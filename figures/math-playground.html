Unit 1 · Functions

1 · the big idea

A function is just a machine

Put a number x in. One rule runs. A number y comes out. "f(x)" just means "machine f, fed x."

2→double it, +1→5
feed x = 2.0 → rule 2x+1 → out comes y = 5.0. The dot is that answer as a point (x, y); all the answers form a line.

2 · domain & range

Which inputs are even allowed?

Domain = the x's you're allowed to feed in. Range = the y's that can come out. Two things break a machine: dividing by zero and square-rooting a negative. Here's f(x) = 1 / (x − c): wherever the bottom hits zero, there's a forbidden wall (dashed) — x can't be there.

bottom is zero when x = c = 1.0, so the domain is "every x except 1.0". The curve never crosses that wall.

3 · straight lines

f(x) = m·x + b

Two numbers make any line. m = slope (how much y climbs per 1 step right). b = where it crosses the y-axis.

step right 1 → climb m = 1.0; crosses y-axis at b = 0.0.

4 · parabolas

f(x) = a·x² + b·x + c

The x² bends the line into a U. a = how tight, and up or down. The roots (orange dots) are where it hits zero — that's all the quadratic formula finds.

Discriminant b²−4ac: positive → 2 roots, zero → 1, negative → floats clear of zero (no real roots). Watch the dots vanish.

5 · bigger polynomials

What the ends do

For high powers, only the top term matters far out. Even power → both ends go the same way (both up, or both down). Odd power → ends go opposite ways. The sign of the lead flips which.

6 · exponential & log

bˣ explodes — log undoes it

bˣ is repeated multiplying (fast growth). logb(x) is the undo button: "what power gives this number?" They mirror across the dashed line y = x.

base b = 2.0: 2³ = 8.0, and the log undoes it: logb(8.0) = 3.

7 · why log rules exist

Log turns × into +

The one superpower of logs: multiplying inside becomes adding outside. log(a·c) = log(a) + log(c). Slide the two numbers and watch the bars literally stack.

Also: log(aʳ) = r·log(a) (a power out front becomes a multiplier) — that's the trick that pulls x down out of an exponent when you solve equations.

8 · trig, finally visual

Where sine & cosine come from

Walk a dot around a circle of radius 1. Its height is sin, its width is cos. As the angle grows, the height traces the wave on the right. That's the whole thing.

at θ = 0.80: sin θ = 0.72 (height), cos θ = 0.70 (width). Notice sin² + cos² = 1 always — it's the circle's radius.

9 · shaping a wave

A·sin(B(x − α)) + C

Four knobs on any wave: A taller/shorter (amplitude), B squished/stretched (period), α slide sideways (phase), C slide up/down.

10 · symmetry

Even, odd, or neither

Even: mirror in the y-axis (left = right), like x². Odd: spin 180° around the origin, like x³. The ghost curve is the mirror — if it lands on the real one, it has that symmetry.

11 · inverse functions

The undo is a mirror in y = x

An inverse swaps input and output. On a graph that's just flipping across the diagonal y = x. (Only works if the function is one-to-one — no two x's share a y.)

12 · one-to-one & onto

The two line tests

One-to-one (injective): a horizontal line never hits twice — every output comes from one input. Onto (surjective): every target height actually gets hit. Drag the horizontal line and count crossings.

Unit 2 · Differential calculus

13 · the limit idea

Secant → tangent

Slope between two points is easy (rise/run). The derivative is what that slope becomes as the second point slides right up next to the first. Slide the gap h toward 0 and watch the grey line (secant) snap onto the orange tangent.

14 · the derivative

Steepness, right here — and f′ as its own curve

Pick a spot; the tilt of the tangent is the derivative there. The green curve below is the slope at every x — the derivative f′. Where green crosses zero, blue has a peak or valley.

(xⁿ)′= n·xⁿ⁻¹  (power rule: bring it down, knock one off)
(sin x)′= cos x  ·  (cos x)′ = −sin x
(eˣ)′= eˣ  ·  (ln x)′ = 1/x
(f·g)′= f′g + fg′  ·  (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g²

15 · the chain rule

A machine feeding a machine

f(g(x)) is two machines in a row. To get the overall rate, you multiply the two rates: how fast the outer reacts to the inner, times how fast the inner reacts to x. Like gears — turn one, the other spins by the product.

Example: (sin(x²))′ = cos(x²) · 2x — outer rate cos(·), times inner rate 2x.

16 · the second derivative

Which way does it cup?

The first derivative is steepness; the second is whether steepness is itself rising or falling — i.e. is the curve a bowl (cups up) or a dome (cups down)? That's the second-derivative test: bowl at a flat spot = minimum, dome = maximum.

Unit 3 · Sequences & series

17 · sequences

Add every step, or multiply every step

A sequence is just a list of numbers with a pattern. Arithmetic: add the same d each time (straight line of dots). Geometric: multiply by the same r each time (dots curve away — that's exponential again).

18 · series

A series is a running total

Add the terms up as you go — the partial sum. For a geometric series with |r| < 1 the pieces shrink so fast the total settles on a number even with infinitely many terms (bars climbing to a ceiling).

Unit 4 · Linear algebra

19 · vectors

A vector is an arrow

Just a direction with a length. Its components are "how far right, how far up." Its length (norm) is the arrow's actual size — Pythagoras: √(x² + y²). Drag the tip.

Drag the orange dot to move the arrow.

20 · the dot product

How much two arrows agree

The dot product measures alignment. Same direction → positive & big. Perpendicular → exactly zero (that's how you test for a right angle). Opposite → negative. Drag either tip.

21 · matrices

A matrix moves all of space

A 2×2 matrix is a recipe for bending the whole grid: scale, rotate, shear, flip. The secret: its two columns are just where the arrows î (right) and ĵ (up) land. The shaded square is the unit square after the move; its area = the determinant. When the grid squashes onto a line, det = 0 and it's not reversible.

22 · solving Ax = b

Two equations = two lines; the answer is where they cross

A 2×2 system is just two straight lines. The solution (x, y) is the single point where they intersect. If you tune them parallel, they never meet → no unique solution, and that's exactly when the determinant is 0.
